Title: Your Thought Patterns Might Be Affecting More Than Your Mood

~1,200 words. Covers the UCL study (300+ participants, tau/amyloid findings), explains RNT, connects to CBT/mindfulness/DBT/EMDR. Written from MMHC's counselor perspective.

Toggle full blog text ▾

Most of us understand that chronic stress is bad for the body. We know it affects sleep, blood pressure, digestion. But what about the brain itself — not just how it feels, but how it physically changes over time? A growing body of research suggests that the way we think — specifically, the patterns we get stuck in — may have consequences far beyond our emotional wellbeing. In 2020, researchers at University College London published a landmark study in Alzheimer's & Dementia that drew a direct line between repetitive negative thinking (RNT) and the biological markers of Alzheimer's disease. The study followed over 300 adults over the age of 55 across a two-year period. Participants who scored higher on measures of repetitive negative thinking showed significantly more cognitive decline. Their memory deteriorated faster. A subset underwent PET brain scans, and those with higher RNT had greater deposits of both tau protein and amyloid — the two proteins most closely associated with Alzheimer's disease. Repetitive negative thinking isn't just having a bad day. It's a pattern — a loop. It's the 2 AM replay of a conversation that went wrong. It's the catastrophic "what if" spiral. As mental health professionals, we see this every day: rumination, chronic worry, cognitive rigidity, self-critical spirals. The biological mechanism: chronic RNT keeps the brain's stress response elevated. Cortisol stays high. Inflammatory markers increase. Over time, this sustained state appears to accelerate the accumulation of neurodegeneration-causing proteins. This is where therapy becomes actionable. CBT helps clients recognize and challenge distorted thought patterns. Mindfulness trains the brain to observe thoughts without getting swept up. DBT teaches distress tolerance. EMDR addresses root events that fuel the loops. "Just think positive" isn't the answer. What works is understanding the pattern, getting support, and building specific skills to relate to your thoughts differently.
